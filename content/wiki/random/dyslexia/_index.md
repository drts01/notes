Since this person is starting from a 3rd-grade level, the focus should be on **multisensory learning**—engaging sight, sound, and touch simultaneously—to bypass the traditional "roadblocks" dyslexia creates in the brain.

Here is a breakdown of tools and techniques tailored for an adult learner.

---

## 1. High-Tech Assistive Tools
These tools remove the immediate barrier of "not being able to read the text" so the person can still access information while they learn.

* **Text-to-Speech (TTS):** Use software like **Speechify** or **NaturalReader**. These highlight words as they are read aloud, which helps the brain connect the visual shape of a word with its sound.
* **Dictation (Speech-to-Text):** Instead of struggling to write, use the "Dictate" feature in Microsoft Word or Google Docs. This allows the person to express complex adult thoughts without being limited by 3rd-grade spelling skills.
* **C-Pen or Scanning Pens:** These are handheld devices that you slide over a line of printed text in a book or on a menu, and it reads the text aloud through earbuds.
* **Specialized Fonts:** Some people find that fonts like **OpenDyslexic** or **Dyslexie** (which have weighted bottoms) prevent the letters from "spinning" or "crowding" on the page.

---

## 2. Evidence-Based Learning Techniques
For someone who was homeschooled without specific resources, the most effective approach is **Structured Literacy**.

* **Orton-Gillingham (OG) Approach:** This is the "gold standard" for dyslexia. It is a highly structured, phonics-based system that teaches the *logic* of the English language rather than asking the student to memorize words.
* **The "Sand Tray" Method:** Even for adults, using tactile feedback helps. Writing a difficult letter or word in a tray of sand or on a textured surface while saying the sound aloud creates a stronger neural pathway.
* **Chunking:** Break words down into syllables or "morphemes" (prefixes, roots, suffixes). Instead of seeing a long word as one scary object, they learn to see three small, manageable pieces.

---

## 3. Adult-Specific Resources
Adults often feel discouraged by "childish" 3rd-grade books. It is important to find **High-Interest, Low-Level (Hi-Lo)** books.

* **Hi-Lo Books:** These are stories written with adult themes (romance, thrillers, workplace drama) but using a simpler vocabulary and sentence structure.
* **Learning Ally:** This is a library of human-read audiobooks specifically for people with dyslexia. Listening while following along with a physical copy of the book is one of the fastest ways to improve "sight word" recognition.
* **Literacy Volunteers of America (LVA):** Many cities have local chapters that provide **free, confidential** one-on-one tutoring for adults. They are trained to work with adult learners and understand the sensitivity of the situation.

---

## 4. Immediate Strategy: The "Whole-Environment" Shift
* **Enable Closed Captioning:** Keep subtitles on for every movie or YouTube video. It provides constant, low-pressure exposure to word-sound association.
* **Audio-First Learning:** Use podcasts and audiobooks to keep building general knowledge and vocabulary. A 3rd-grade reading level does not mean 3rd-grade intelligence; keeping the mind sharp with complex ideas makes the motivation to read much higher.

### A Note on Progress
At this stage, the most important thing is to move away from the "look and say" method (guessing words by their shape) and toward **phonemic awareness** (understanding the sounds that make up words). A professional evaluation by a neuropsychologist or a specialized reading tutor can provide a specific "map" of where their phonetic gaps are.
