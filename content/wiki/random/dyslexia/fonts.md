While there isn't a single "best" font for everyone with dyslexia, two open-source options are currently leading the conversation for different reasons.

### 1. Lexend: The Modern, Research-Backed Choice
**Lexend** is widely considered the most effective modern open-source font. Unlike older dyslexia fonts, it was designed based on the theory that "visual crowding" (letters being too close together) is the primary barrier to reading.

* **Why it works:** It uses expanded character spacing and a high "x-height" (taller lowercase letters). This reduces the effort your brain spends "decoding" the shape of the word, allowing you to focus on the meaning.
* **Why choose it:** Choose Lexend if you find that standard text looks "cramped" or if you want a font that looks professional and modern. It is available for free on **Google Fonts** and is becoming a standard in many educational apps.

### 2. OpenDyslexic: The Specialized Choice
**OpenDyslexic** is the most famous "dyslexia-specific" font. Its letters have "heavy weighted bottoms" to provide a sense of gravity to each character.

* **Why it works:** The theory is that the weighted bottoms prevent the brain from "flipping" or "rotating" letters (like turning a **b** into a **d**).
* **Why choose it:** Choose this if you specifically struggle with letter rotation or "moving text." However, be aware that research is mixed; while some people find it life-changing, others find the unique shapes distracting.

---

### Comparison: Which one should you use?

| Feature | Lexend | OpenDyslexic |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Goal** | Reducing "crowding" and visual stress. | Preventing letter "flipping" and rotation. |
| **Appearance** | Clean, sans-serif, looks like a "normal" font. | Unique, "bottom-heavy" shapes; looks distinct. |
| **Best For** | Long-form reading and professional use. | Beginners or those with severe spatial confusion. |
| **Versatility** | Excellent; works well for everyone. | Polarizing; some love it, others find it "busy." |

---

### The "Familiarity" Factor (Don't Ignore These)
Surprisingly, some research suggests that **standard, familiar fonts** can be just as effective because the brain is already "trained" to recognize them. If the specialized fonts don't feel right, try these open-source classics:
* **Andika:** Specifically designed for literacy and beginning readers.
* **Comic Neue:** A "cleaned up" version of Comic Sans. Many people with dyslexia prefer it because every letter shape is distinct (the **a** looks different from the **o**, etc.).

### Pro-Tip for an Adult Learner
Since this person is at a 3rd-grade reading level, the **spacing** often matters more than the **font**. Whatever font they choose, try these settings in their browser or word processor:
1.  **Increase line spacing** to 1.5 or 2.0.
2.  **Increase letter spacing** (tracking).
3.  **Avoid Italics,** which are notoriously difficult for dyslexic readers to process.
